Years ago I found some short animations depicting various dinosaurs wielding different weapons; there was even an obvious reference to Zoro among them. Not long after, I found a Steam page for a game all about dinos with swords. It had an uncertain release date and was being made by that same animator, who posted occasional updates on social media. I joined JeanAnimate's Discord server for the game, and when he sent out a call for playtesters for the full game, I was hesitant at first (people are scary) but decided to go with it… mostly just to be able to play the full game early.

That is to say, Dinoblade was an ambitious project by someone who was more animator than programmer. As more of a programmer myself, it was inspiring to see, and his background as an animator was for better or worse evident throughout both the playtest and my actual playthrough of the game.

Dinoblade knows exactly what it is: a game about dinosaurs with swords, and it commits to that idea completely. Anyone who's sold on the premise alone will find something to love here.

Even if you're like me and haven't actually played most of FromSoftware's library, the formula will feel familiar: our protagonist is dropped into the middle of nowhere, the only choice being to pick up the weapon that's provided to them. The starting area gives the energy of what most would associate with a "prehistoric" world. It's all effectively a straight line, but smaller details do work to immerse early in the world itself. You'll often find groups of enemies mingling among themselves before noticing you. Some might even ignore you entirely unless you engage.

[Insert Image of Tiny Tank]

Our Spinosaurus [whom I've creatively aliased as Spiny] carries a greatsword in his jaw, and with it he performs the typical souls moveset: a simple light attack combo along with a more drawn-out heavy attack chain. Spiny also manages to move that hulking figure enough to dodge attacks, but more importantly he's able to both block and parry with his blade. 

Jean mentioned during the beta that the mechanic itself is based on Sekiro, and it shows in that parrying enemies is not only satisfying in succession, but can also become integral to certain playstyles. A perfect parry can prevent damage, but landing them consistently is what really rewards you: they can lead to instantly killing overworld enemies, or dealing a punishing counterattack to bosses. Parrying also replenishes the game's version of magic, called "Resonance," which certain skills require.

I personally found myself struggling to truly master the mechanic, but the learning curve itself is enjoyable for someone who rarely plays these types of games. And if you've already danced to this song many times before, the animations that accompany the parry are both nice to see and have enough weight behind them to still feel as satisfying as the genre's better-known titles. However, the fact that I can do alright with it should let you know that the parry windows can be pretty generous, though not as free as the spectacle-based action games I play more often. But unlike those titles mashing only makes parries harder, which I may have realized the hard way.

But it just wouldn't be soulslike without huge walls to block your progress. There are two camps of bosses: (kinda) optional enemies aptly named after the weapon they use, and large dinos sharing the name "Tyrant" whom I question if they even need one. These boss fights test everything learned about the games mechanics thus far, but also how adaptable your playstyle is as many can afford to apply more "unorthodox" tactics due to their larger size (and some of them are just cheating). I'd call these fights generally "fair": soulslike novices may get intimidated, while veteran instincts give you more time to enjoy how everything looks. And for that, after each Tyrant the player is rewarded with a knew weapon and extra use on their heal.

The smaller bosses aren't just more skill checks, however, as each will still reward you with something called "boss SP." This limited resource is your means of unlocking and upgrading your skills, so hunting down every source of it is recommended (and fair warning: as far as I know, not enough of it exists in a single playthrough to unlock everything). And going back to Resonance, each boss defeated grants you an equippable "summon," a cheap skill that calls the beast for a single high-damage strike. But the real draw is that each one has a passive ability that aids in combat: extending parry windows, healing whenever you block, even something as simple as increasing damage. Despite the actual "RPG" side being relatively simple, with pretty barebones stats and skill trees, summons add a type of build variety I didn't expect once I began engaging with the game more. I'm usually the type to go all in on healing and stamina maintenance, but I actually did find myself switching between summons regularly. 

[Insert Kira, The Exile; "I can see why you were exiled."]

The bosses themselves I almost universally enjoyed due to the energy that goes into each moveset without many of them being able to utter a word. Some fight like the starving beasts they are, while others appear more methodical in their fighting styles. I wouldn't say any of the bosses I encountered strictly fight like the dinosaur they're supposed to be (they just give some of them earthbending), so it's clear the animation came from someone having fun.
